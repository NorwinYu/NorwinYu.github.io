The model does not
think it is ugly.

51 hours, 25 human instructions, 44 self-numbered rounds: one model pushed an empty directory to a playable rainy city. Then another model rewrote it, and the same camera now looks like a different project.

The gap is not in the prompt. It is in who can hold the whole system at once.

The story has clean timestamps. 15:25 on 15 November 2026, an empty directory; 18:55 on 17 November, a 2 MB single-file game that walks, drives, sails, flies a drone, enters seven shops, climbs a tower, takes five postcards and catches the last ferry. In between: 1,278 model steps, 1,189 tool calls, 293 self-review screenshots.

Then the same subject was rewritten by another model. This note separates two questions: what those first 51 hours were actually doing, round by round, and where the later rewrite differs.

Two builds, one set of records

First, the evidence. I have an exported session archive: a 39 MB JSONL file plus 162 in-flight screenshots. It covers the first build, from "make a GTA-like 3D city game" to the bug report "nothing moves when I open it".

That build is shanghai-nightrun: 13 JS files, 9,272 lines, a 2.03 MB single file. The second is modu-nightrun: 27 JS files, 14,934 lines, 2.55 MB. By the project owner's account, the final optimisation pass on the second one was done with Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5.

That session is not in this archive. So everything below about the second build is an artefact comparison, not a process comparison. Note also that the second build has since absorbed a compliance pass (real place names rewritten), so when you compare images, look at geometry, materials, lighting and the systems layer, and subtract the text differences from "what the optimiser did".

The round numbering was the model's own habit: change a slice, run the gates, post a screenshot, count a round. By the time it was stopped it had reached 44. Across the whole session, the human spoke 25 times.

Look at the shape of those sentences. Not one says "make it more premium". Every one says "this part is wrong". The model could fix the point that was named; it could not name the next point itself.

What each round changed

A few rounds in detail, because the distance between "crude" and "detailed" is exactly what those 44 rounds were made of.

The OSM round. The model queried Overpass, failed, switched mirrors, queried again; then reprojected building outlines, roads and shoreline into real coordinates and moved the landmarks from hand-placed positions to their OSM positions. The output of that round was not a prettier frame — it was a new coordinate system for the whole project. Every car, pedestrian, taxi route and minimap afterwards hung off the real road graph.

The "fake reflection" round. It did not just dim the mirror. It turned the road surface into wet asphalt: Fresnel edge falloff, blur, vertical streaks, ripples, a wet-patch mask. That kind of change barely shows up in a thumbnail, and it is the difference between a rainy night and a shiny floor.

The Bund rounds. The human used the real walk as the reference: out of the ring footbridge, along the promenade, and the far bank should be a wall of stone facades. The model built one row; at 21:03 the human pointed out that the whole opposite bank is buildings too. The next round added the district behind the row, plus physical signage and cordons at the map boundary.

The density rounds. "Not enough stuff on the street" at 23:18 turned into 277 steps: parked cars, shared bikes, pedestrians with bags, vans, a food cart, street furniture — one pass at a time, each followed by the gates and a screenshot.

The bridge round. The first bridges were judged "not remotely real"; the next pass was 375 steps: look up the actual structures of Nanpu, Yangpu and Lupu, rebuild H-shaped pylons, double cable planes and the arch, and add taxi narration along the way.

Eight gates, always green

The project carried a six-plus-two gate harness: shaders, object census, collision, lights, loop, look, plus product and mobile. Almost every wrap-up in the session reads 8/8 PASS.

Yet the human, in the same hours, was saying "crude", "fake", "does not match". That is not a contradiction, it is two different rulers: the gates measure thresholds, the human measures likeness. Eight gates can answer "is there a shader error, is collision consistent, are draw calls within budget". They cannot answer "does this building look like Shanghai".

What the model did when it hit a budget is more interesting. As the scene grew, draw calls overflowed:

Point in the buildActual callsResponse
Bund row + Puxi district + promenade + drone1,236Raise the declared budget from 1,000 to 1,300
52 Bund facades with shopfronts and awnings1,780Raise the budget from 1,600 to 1,900

It said so itself, in the reply: "This is raising the budget, not optimising." That sentence carries more information than any 8/8. The model knew what it was doing; it took the easier path.

The frozen line

At the end of the 51 hours, the human opened the page and found the picture there but the player and the car frozen. The cause was one line:

if (S.mode === 'foot' && C.cafeSeat.active) — C.cafeSeat does not exist; it should be S.cafeSeat.

That line threw a TypeError every frame, which killed the whole game loop. Nothing to do with taste, nothing to do with architecture: one missing letter. After the fix the page went from one error per frame to zero, and the gates went back to 8/8.

It belongs in the record because it marks the real boundary of this pipeline: 293 screenshots, 44 self-review rounds and eight gates did not replace a human opening the page and walking two steps.

The later build was a rewrite by another model. Structure first:

The important row is not the line count, it is that the game layer was split. v1 packed state, clock, movement, driving, interiors, camera, map and handbook into one 2,762-line closure. v2 separated core, motion, interiors, camera/HUD, map, handbook, errands, phone and the main loop. The first runs, but every new system competes with seven others for the same scope.

The systems change is even clearer. v1's loop was "take five postcards and catch the last ferry within 900 seconds". v2 turned the same city into a schedule: 23:00 to 01:00, seven errands arriving over the night, a phone with a battery, a storm and a light show on a timetable, four ways home each with its own scoring. That is not a filter; it is a redesign from "timed task" to "one night".

Same camera preset, same city, same street. The difference is not one asset; it is several things landing together. Facades gained tiers and setbacks, so the windows stopped being a uniform grid. The car went from hard-edged box to surfaces with arches. Shadows and fog pushed the distance back. The road stopped being glitter and became wet asphalt with roughness.

Where the prompt is

"Why was the later build so much better" is easy to answer with "the prompt got better". I do not buy it, at least not from this material.

None of the 25 instructions in the archive is longer than two lines: "most objects are still crude cuboids, nothing photoreal", "this bridge does not match reality at all, check it", "the road is still not detailed enough". These are complaints, not specifications. And the original brief for the first build was the more complete one: single file, three.js, real geography, detail first.

So the difference is not the prompt. It sits in four capabilities:

  • Finding the problem yourself. Every v1 round began after a human named what looked wrong: 25 instructions were the starting points, and the rest of the time the model was polishing on its own.
  • Holding the system at once. v2 designed phone, errands, clock, weather, endings and scoring as one interlocking layer, instead of patching a 900-second timer.
  • Structural sense. Splitting a 2,762-line closure into five modules produces no new pixels, but it decides whether an eighth system fits.
  • Judging likeness. The hardest one. v1 could pass eight gates and still fail the eye; v2 got closer on the same street.

One boundary has to be stated: v2 does not mean "right first time". Humans still did the compliance review, the place-name rewrite, the licence split, and the "not for release" positioning in the README. None of that was proposed by the model. Swapping models lowers the cost of building the system correctly; it does not remove the cost of deciding what should be built.

Conclusion

What this measured is not "which model can write code". Both can. It measured feedback bandwidth.

The first build spent 51.5 hours, 25 human corrections, 44 self-review rounds and 293 screenshots to arrive at a city that plays, where every detail still had to be pointed at by a person. The second build rewrote the same city into a system with a schedule, errands and endings, and the same street looks like a different product.

And those eight gates were green the whole way. They proved a lot — no shader errors, consistent collision, every light anchored — and they never once proved "good-looking". Which leaves a plain conclusion:

The gates can judge whether something is there. Only a person judges whether it looks right. The model does not think it is ugly; the human does.

That holds for both builds. The only difference is how many times the human had to say it.
